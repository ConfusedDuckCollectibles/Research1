# Exact holes in a named 24-cell inside a five-dimensional kissing code

Private research companion, 25 November 2026. The following bounds are for a named equatorial template, not the unrestricted kissing number.

**Two holes.** If a kissing code contains exactly 22 vertices of a named regular equatorial 24-cell, it has at most 38 points. Equality occurs exactly when one deletes those two named equator vertices from a completed D5 or Leech L5 arrangement with that equator.

**Three holes without a triangle.** If the code contains exactly 21 vertices of the named cell and the three missing roots do not form a triangle of pairwise contact edges, it has at most 37 points. This is sharp for every such triple, covering 1,928 of the 2,024 triples. For every triangular hole triple there is a 38-point construction. No triangular-case upper bound is proved here.

In particular, a 39-point code with at least 22 vertices of a named 24-cell actually contains at least 23. For 40 points the existing one-hole rigidity theorem gives the complete equator and D5 or L5.

## Scope and prior work

This strengthens [Research1 #24](https://github.com/ConfusedDuckCollectibles/Research1/blob/83a61b036ad943b1297091922d823758b4b1e019/discoveries/kissing-five-partial-24-cell/CHECKPOINT.md), which bounded every 22-root extension by 40 and proved rigidity at 40 for 23 retained. Its single-root and adjacent-pair cone geometry is rederived below and extended to nontriangular triples. [Cohn–Rajagopal, §2](https://arxiv.org/html/2412.00937v3) describe D5, L5 and their triality frames, as well as the two other known nonisometric 40-point configurations, Q5 and R5. [wustep's August 17 log](https://github.com/wustep/maths/blob/main/problems/kissing-5d/ATTACK.md) already gives the full D4-containing ≤40 obstruction. Its August 27 q4 statement about “22 or more D5-type points” is a different, finite quarter-grid restriction (and excludes 41, not 39), not a continuous exact-hole theorem. The general nonpositive Gram decomposition used below is classical; see [King–Mixon–Parshall–Wells, Theorem 2](https://arxiv.org/html/2008.12871v3). No matching statement for the exact two- or three-hole continuum questions was found in a bounded search. [Tomar's current exact-certificate preprint](https://www.mefyi.com/papers/kissing-numbers-five-seven/) still gives the best located unrestricted upper bound 43; the prior refereed bound is [Mittelmann–Vallentin's 44](https://arxiv.org/abs/0902.1105).

## 1. Setup and triality

Scale to squared norm 2 and distinct-point inner products at most 1. The named 24-cell is D={±e_i±e_j:i<j≤4} in the equator of R^5. Let M⊂D be exactly the missing equatorial roots, with k=|M| equal to two or three. “Exactly” means X∩D=D\M: we impose no exclusion of other equatorial points. Write extra points as (u,t), τ=|t|, high for τ≥1 and low for τ<1. Put t=0 only on the upper side, so each extra is counted once. Reflection lets us analyze either side at height τ≥0.

The full polar inequalities in the horizontal four-space are |u_i|+|u_j|≤1. Their domain has ||u||≤1: for a=max|u_i|≤1/2 this is immediate; for a≥1/2 the squared norm is at most a²+3(1−a)²≤1. Equality gives exactly the 24 unit directions F consisting of coordinate axes and half-cube directions (±1/2)^4. They split into three 8-point orthoplexes: coordinate, even-sign and odd-sign. Within a frame dot products between distinct vectors are 0 or −1; across frames absolute dot products are 1/2. The signed coordinate symmetries and the normalized symmetric Hadamard matrix with columns (++ ++), (++−−), (+−+−), (+−−+) act on D and permute these three frames as the full symmetric group. In particular any of the frames can be sent to the coordinate axes. In fact this Hadamard matrix exchanges coordinate and even frames, while a one-coordinate sign flip exchanges half-cube parity.

Every low has ||u||>1, so it strictly violates some missing-root inequality r·u>1. Assign it to a missing root at which r·u is maximal, breaking ties arbitrarily but once. A low equal to a named missing root is excluded by the exact-hole hypothesis.

## 2. All low cones for the two theorems

Two nonadjacent distinct roots cannot be violated together with at most three holes. If their dot is ≤−1, use ||r+s||≤√2 and ||u||≤√2. For dot zero there are two alternative disjoint decompositions of r+s into pairs of D-roots other than r,s. On equal support, change the canceled coordinate to either of the two unused coordinates; on disjoint support, use the other two pairings of the four signed coordinates. At most one further hole cannot hit both pairs, and a retained pair forces (r+s)·u≤2. Thus, if M is nontriangular, any low violates only its assigned root or that root and one adjacent hole. This also covers every pair M.

For r∈M form the cone generated by the apex (r,0), the six rays (v,1) for v∈F with v·r=1, and one ray
    W_{r,s}=((r+s)/2,1/√2)
for each s∈M with r·s=1. Everything is intrinsic under symmetries of D.

If only r is violated, normalize r=e1+e2. With p=u1+u2>1 and m=max(|u3|,|u4|), the eight obeyed cross inequalities e_i±e_j, i=1,2 and j=3,4, give 0<u1,u2≤1 and m≤1−max(u1,u2). These imply τ≥2−p: if p<2 write u=(p−1)r+(2−p)h, where h1+h2=1, h1,h2≥0, |h3|,|h4|≤min(h1,h2). This base is the convex hull of e1,e2 and the four vectors (1/2,1/2,±1/2,±1/2), by splitting weight 2 min(h1,h2) into the square and the rest into an axis. The same convex combination of lifted generators has height 2−p and norm ≤√2. At p=2 one has the apex. In particular τ≥|u1−u2|+2m. Whenever this last inequality holds and τ>0, taking A=(p−τ)/2>0 and H=(u−Ar)/τ places H in the same base, giving (u,τ)=A(r,0)+τ(H,1).

If both r,s are violated, signed permutations give r=e1+e2, s=e1+e3. Put u=(a,b,c,d), κ=1−a. The maximal assignment to r and the obeyed constraints give a≥b≥c>κ≥0, b+c≤1 and |d|≤κ. Indeed the coordinate-4 bounds and the b+c inequality are obeyed, and a+c>1 and b+c≤1 imply a>b. A third hole causes no issue because its inequality, whether retained or not, is obeyed. For h∈[2κ,2c], take coefficients
    b−c, 2c−h, a−b−c+h/2, (h+2d)/2, (h−2d)/2
on the rays (r,0), W_{r,s}, (e1,1), and H_±=((1/2,1/2,1/2,±1/2),1), respectively. All are nonnegative, and their horizontal combination is u. The height T(h) increases, with slope 3/2−1/√2 and end value T(2c)=a−b+2c. The coefficients sum to one at h=2κ, so T(2κ)≤τ by convexity of the norm. Interpolate to obtain the actual height if τ≤T(2c); otherwise τ>|a−b|+2max(|c|,|d|) and the original seven rays suffice. This includes the genuine equatorial boundary if feasible, with no gridding.

The rays have norm √2 and pairwise dot ≥1. The seven old-ray products follow in r-normal coordinates; W_{r,s} has dot >1 with all of them (for example its smallest horizontal product with a base lift is 1/2, and its height contributes 1/√2). Two W rays for the same r have dot 3/2+(s·t)/4≥5/4, since s and t cannot be antipodes when both have dot 1 with r. For a cone point on the sphere the coefficient sum is ≥1, strictly >1 if distinct generators have positive coefficients, by the triangle inequality and its strictness for nonparallel vectors. Distinct low single rays are the apex and W rays, whose pairwise products are strictly >1. Hence **one low per assigned root per side**, without assuming the lows are on a discrete shell.

## 3. How a high point prevents a low type

We use this only for height-one highs. A polar high (w,1) has w∈F; if w·r=1 it blocks every nonapex r-low. To check this, send w's frame to coordinate axes so w is one of the signed coordinates of r. The high dot with every old generator is at least 1, and its dot with W_{r,s} is >1: w·r=1, w·s∈{0,1}, and 1/√2 adds to the horizontal average. A multiray low has coefficient sum >1, and W itself conflicts. The apex is absent from an exact-hole code.

If a height-one high (w,1) is nonpolar, choose its assigned maximal missing-root violation r. It blocks all lows of this type. For a sole violation write w=(a,b,c,d) in r-normal coordinates. Then a,b>0, a+b>1 and |c|+|d|≤2(1−max(a,b))<1. For a double violation use the normalized mixed chamber from Section 2, so a≥b≥c>κ≥0 and |d|≤κ. In either case a+b>|c|+|d|, so the high dots strictly above 1 with the apex, two axial lifts and four half-cube lifts. For *any* deleted neighbor s of r, including one obeyed by this high, w·s≥0: every r-adjacent root is e_i±e_j with i=1,2, j=3,4; in the sole case its dot is at least a+b−1>0, and in the mixed case at least min(b−c,b−|d|)≥0. Therefore the high dots with W_{r,s} at least (w·r)/2+1/√2>1. The coefficient-sum bound proves the claimed conflict.

## 4. Saturated and nearly saturated high layers

A pole u=0 conflicts with every other high on its side. With no poles, normalize the horizontal directions; they are pairwise nonpositive since ττ'≥1. Their Gram matrix has rank ≤4. Split the vertices into components joined by its strictly negative entries. Distinct components are orthogonal. Each component has nullity at most one: I−G on a component is irreducible nonnegative (a singleton is nonsingular), and if the block is singular its eigenvalue 1 is largest and simple by Perron–Frobenius. Every singular component thus has at least two points, and a size-two singular component is an antipodal pair. At least m−4 components are singular with m highs. At m≥6 there are at least two components, so every point has an orthogonal neighbor. Their original heights have product ≤1 and individually ≥1; hence all these highs are at height one. Rankin's bound gives m≤8 (also: m−4≤m/2). At m=8 the decomposition is four antipodal pairs; at m=7 at least two such pairs exist.

**Eight highs.** With no antipodal pair of deleted roots, a ± horizontal pair is compatible with all template roots since each opposite root pair has a retained representative. If there is an antipodal hole pair ±r, it is the only one for k≤3. Among the four unit axes of the high layer only one can violate |w·r|≤1 because Σ_axes(w·r)²=2. The other three axes belong to F; mutually orthogonal axes of F lie in one triality frame, so the fourth line is the fourth axis of that frame, also polar. A full high layer is thus always one of the three frames. In its axial coordinates every root has a matching signed axis. Section 3 rules out all lows on that side.

**Seven highs.** If some antipodal direction pair is nonpolar, its directions violate at least one root each, so they ban two opposite hole types. There cannot be a second type violated on either end because its opposite would require a fourth hole. There are no remaining lows for k=2 and at most one for k=3.

Otherwise select two polar antipodal pairs. They are orthogonal, hence in a single triality frame; send them to ±e1,±e2. The three remaining unit high directions lie on the e3,e4 circle. A nonaxis direction there violates exactly the root of its open sign quadrant. There is at most one direction per open quadrant by nonpositivity. Any root involving coordinate 1 or 2 is already blocked by a matching high axis. A circle axis blocks the two quadrants matching its signed coordinate. If all three circle highs are axes, their blocking covers every quadrant. If there is one nonaxis, the two available axes must be opposite its two signed coordinates; they cover the other three quadrants and the nonaxis bans its own. If there are two nonaxes, both must occupy the two open quadrants on the opposite side of the remaining axis, and each bans its own. If all three are nonaxes, the three banned roots must be all three holes (and are impossible with two holes). Thus in this second case no lows remain.

**Six highs.** A nonpolar high bans a type, leaving at most k−1 lows. If all six highs are polar and lack antipodal pairs, at least two singular components must have ≥3 members each. They are exactly two triples. In a polar triple with no antipodes, off-diagonal nonpositive dots are 0 or −1/2, so the Gram is singular only when all three are −1/2 (expand the determinant). Such a triple uses all three frames, hence no polar vector can be orthogonal to all its members. This contradicts the other component. Thus six polar highs have an antipodal pair and all the other polar directions are in its frame by orthogonality. In that frame the only possible unblocked root must use the two absent signed axes. This yields at most one low. This reasoning does not assume that a seven- or six-point high layer is always polar.

## 5. Counting, sharp examples, and the triangular boundary

For two holes a side with ≤5 highs has at most 7 extras, with six highs at most 7 by Section 4, with seven highs exactly seven, and with eight highs exactly eight. Hence at most 22+8+8=38. At equality both high layers are full frames and there are no lows. Reinsert the two named roots, which are compatible with both frames by polarity, yielding precisely a completed D5 or L5 arrangement as described in §1 and by Cohn–Rajagopal. Conversely both constructions attain the bound for any choice of the two named deletions.

For three nontriangular holes, a side with ≤5 highs has at most 8 extras, with six highs at most 8, with seven highs at most 8, and with eight highs exactly eight. Thus at most 21+8+8=37. Deleting those roots from D5 attains 37 for every usable triple. The adjacency edge counts among the 2,024 unordered triples are respectively 488, 1,056, 384 and 96 for zero, one, two, and three edges.

The omitted 96 triangular triples genuinely differ. For the canonical face triangle {e1+e2,e1+e3,e2+e3}, delete those roots from D5 and add (1,3/5,4/5,0,0). It has squared norm 2, violates exactly those three named roots, and its absolute coordinates are at most one so all 16 axial caps remain compatible. Every adjacent triangle of roots is signed-coordinate-equivalent to that face triangle or to the star triangle {e1+e2,e1+e3,e1+e4}: the three distinct coordinate supports intersect pairwise; common coordinate signs must agree. Apply the normalized Hadamard of §1 after flipping the first coordinate to send the star to the negatives of the face roots on coordinates {2,3,4}. This preserves D, so the 38 construction transports to every triangle. We do not infer an upper bound for the omitted class.
