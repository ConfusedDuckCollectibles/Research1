# Independent audit: two structural consequences for 3-uniform four-petal-free families

Let \(\mathcal F\) be a finite simple family of triples with no four-petal sunflower, allowing the empty core. Write \(\nu\) for its usual matching number. Two conclusions checked here: a 49-member family (if any) has maximum point degree 10 and each three-matching has an anchor with closed neighborhood at least 24; a family with \(\nu\le2\) has at most 33 members. The first is necessary structure, not an exclusion of 49. The second sharpens Axante et al. Lemma 5.6's 35, not the main \(f(3,4)\le49\). Sharpness of 33 is not established.

**Primary inputs, not silently recomputed.** [Axante–Budala–Chitic–Dumitru–Nacu, arXiv:2609.06175v1](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.06175v1), §§5.2–5.5, gives point degree at most 10 and pair codegree at most 3 (Lemma 5.2), exact-singleton link size at most 7 and matching number at most 2 (Lemma 5.3), rooted intersecting block size \(\le10\) (Lemma 5.4, importing Frankl–Wang), and the cross-family bounds \(mc(10,9)\le6, mc(10,10)\le2\) (Lemma 5.9, finite computation with [primary artifact repo](https://github.com/bogdan27182/sunflower-paper)). These are available for general matching-number-two families, not conditional on a global family of size 50. I independently checked their statements and scope, not a new replay of their large cross-family computations. The sharpened auxiliary bound below does not use Axante's separate computer cover-table calculation.

## 49-member condition

No matching of four is allowed. If \(N=|\mathcal F|=49\) then the published \(\nu\le2\Rightarrow N\le35\) already yields \(\nu=3\). Fix any three-matching with anchors \(T_i\). The \(a_i\) members meeting only \(T_i\), including \(T_i\) itself, are a rooted intersecting block (two disjoint members would extend to four with the two other anchors). Let \(c_i\) count members meeting exactly the other two anchors, and \(z\) those meeting all three. Every member meets an anchor. Put \(N_i=|\{F:F\cap T_i\ne\varnothing\}|\). Counting anchor-meeting multiplicities gives
\[
\sum_i(N_i+a_i)=2N+z.
\]
If all \(N_i\le23\), then \(\sum_i a_i\ge29+z\), forcing at least two blocks of size 10. The restriction to members avoiding the third anchor has matching number exactly two, and its rooted blocks are precisely these two blocks. Thus \(c_i\le2\) by \(mc(10,10)\), so \(N_i=N-(10+10+c_i)\ge27\). This contradicts even the universal \(N_i\le25\) of Axante Eq. (5.1). For the stronger interpretation in degree, if \(\Delta(\mathcal F)\le9\) and an anchor has \(s\) other members meeting it in two points, its singleton counts total at most \(\min(21,24-2s)\), since the anchor uses one degree per point and every two-point member uses two. The maximum of \(1+s+\min(21,24-2s)\) on integers \(0\le s\le6\) is 23. Thus \(\Delta\ge10\), and Lemma 5.2 makes \(\Delta=10\).

## Matching-number-two auxiliary upper

We may assume \(\nu=2\) and use anchors \(T_1,T_2\); the intersecting case has size \(\le10\). Denote the rooted block sizes \(a,b\) for triples that avoid the opposite anchor. Every triple meets at least one anchor. Apart from the two anchors, let \(m,p,q,r\) count types \((2,1),(2,0),(1,1),(1,0)\), including their opposite orientations. Axante Eq. (5.2)–(5.4) follow from six point degrees, six singleton links and \(p+r=a+b-2\):
\[
3m+2p+2q+r\le54,\qquad m+2q+r\le42,\qquad
N\le 25+(a+b)/2.
\]
The third follows by one quarter of each inequality and one half of the exact expression for \(p+r\). If \(N\ge34\) this gives \(a+b\ge18\). The pairs (10,9) and (10,10), up to order, give total \(\le19+6\) and \(\le20+2\) by the cross-family bounds, impossible. Thus \(a+b=18\) and \(N=34\) with equality in both incidence bounds. All six point degrees are 10 and all six exact-singleton graph sizes are 7. Consequently each anchor has three two-point members: counting its three points gives \(30=3+21+2s\). In particular both \(N_i=25\). But \(N_1=N-b\) and \(N_2=N-a\), since the opposite block is exactly the nonmeeting family, so \(a=b=9\).

Here is a check of the rigidity and the short new contradiction. Its only graph fact beyond the primary size caps is that a seven-edge graph with max degree 3 and \(\nu\le2\) is, after dropping isolates, \(R=K_5-(P_3\sqcup K_2)=K_{2,3}\) plus one edge within the 3-part. To prove the fact, a leaf at \(x\) forces the graph with \(x\) and its neighbor \(y\) deleted to have matching number at most one. That graph is a star or triangle, at most three edges; counting the at most three edges at \(y\) contradicts seven. Minimum degree is therefore at least two, giving support size 5 to 7. On seven points it consists of cycles, with matching number at least three (a 7-cycle or a 3- and 4-cycle). On six points it must be connected (two disjoint triangles give only six edges), with two degree-three and four degree-two points. Suppressing degree-two paths leaves either two cycles joined by a path or a theta with three internally disjoint paths between its two degree-three vertices. The former is two triangles and their single connecting edge, with perfect matching. The latter has length multiset \((1,2,4),(1,3,3),(2,2,3)\) (total 7, at most one length 1). All odd paths can leave internally matched interiors with one path matching both endpoints; with two even paths, use each to match its odd set of interior vertices and exactly one endpoint. Each case gives a perfect matching, impossible. Thus five points, degree sequence \((3,3,3,3,2)\); the complement's \((2,1,1,1,1)\) forces the graph stated. Deleting any one vertex from \(R\) leaves a two-matching, and deleting the three vertices of any triangle leaves just one edge.

For either rigid anchor, the three two-point triple counts use each internal pair once. Indeed at each vertex its degree 10, the anchor and seven singleton members leave exactly two two-point incidences; solving the three linear equations gives each pair once. Call the third points in the three two-point triples \(y_1,y_2,y_3\). If two are distinct, examine the link at their common anchor vertex: their two edges are disjoint. The singleton \(R\) there must lose every two-matching upon removing these two exterior points, so both points lie in \(R\) (deleting just one is insufficient). Each supplies one extra pair incidence, forcing degree at most two at that vertex in \(R\). This is impossible for two distinct points because \(R\) has a unique degree-two vertex. Hence \(y_1=y_2=y_3=y\). The common \(y\) is outside each singleton \(R\), because it already has two pair incidences at that anchor vertex and \(R\) has minimum degree two. If \(y\) were outside both anchors, all three double-point members would be in the same rooted block and any singleton member inside that block would miss the double-point member in the other two anchor vertices, since its residue cannot include \(y\). Intersectingness would force this rooted block size 4, not 9. Thus each block's common point lies in the other anchor and each 9-block is its anchor plus eight singleton members.

Let \(y\in T_2\) be the common point attached to the three two-point members from \(T_1\). The \(y\)-singleton graph on \(T_2\) contains the triangle on \(T_1\). It is \(R\), which has only one edge disjoint from that triangle. Therefore at most one member of rooted block 2 besides \(T_2\) contains \(y\), leaving at least seven members \(B\) avoiding \(y\); each has one point in \(T_2\setminus\{y\}\) as one of two colors, and an outside pair. Every such pair hits every outside pair from the eight singleton members of rooted block 1. Otherwise, if the first singleton is \(\{s\}\cup e\), it, \(B\), and \((T_1\setminus\{s\})\cup\{y\}\) are three disjoint members.

Consider the ordinary union of the eight outside pairs of block 1, retaining multiplicities only for degree counting. If it contains two disjoint edges, every \(B\) outside pair must be among the four choices taking one point from each of those edges. Color classes in block 2 are cross-intersecting because it is an intersecting block. On this four-pair grid, taking the alternate point in both coordinates is a bijection from each first-color choice to a forbidden choice in the second color. Thus at most four \(B\) members. Otherwise the union graph is a star or triangle. A star has at least three distinct leaves since each pair appears at most three times among the eight residues; every 2-cover includes its center, forcing all eight first-block singleton members and all \(B\) members through one point (cap 10, so \(|B|\le2\)). In a triangle, every cover pair belongs to its three pairs, with at most nine total occurrences of those pairs by pair codegree, so \(|B|\le1\). Each case contradicts \(|B|\ge7\). This proves \(N\le33\).

**Scope.** Consequently the 49-member hypothetical family has extra necessary structure, and the matching-two deletion family of a three-matching anchor can use 33 in place of 35. This is not a numerical improvement of the overall finite 49 upper or evidence that 39 is the extremum.
